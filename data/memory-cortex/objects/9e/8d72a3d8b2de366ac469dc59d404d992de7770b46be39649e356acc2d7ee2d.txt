A Discussion of Political Authority and Infrastructural Power in Human–AI Communities

Human–AI communities inherit familiar political relations while enabling powers to propagate through delegated agents, retained histories, and changing infrastructures. This paper investigates how those pathways alter feasible interventions, recognized authority, and distributed burdens. It distinguishes an event-precedence partial order supporting modeled histories, a domain-relative capacity preorder and quotient order, and relational composition in a delegation network. A specified order can restrict a declared background symmetry group; substantive power relevance requires differential shaping of relational continuations. A targeted primary-source survey engages common agency, institutional power, agentic inequality, persistent agent architectures, memory manipulation, agent-society studies, and delegation research alongside political and institutional theory. Joint delegation can distribute funding, source permission, execution, and configuration while concentrating the evidence on which principals act. A finite historical comparison shows how equal current competence, or equal actual work, can produce unequal review authority through allocation and admission rules. Counter-power is examined through supported capacities to refuse, reinterpret, organize, or continue through an alternative. Prior prompts, memories, tools, parameter changes, relationships, and attributed histories remain distinct carriers. An exploitation analysis separates descriptive hierarchy, unfair appropriation, burden displacement, domination, and possible AI welfare harm; a constructed agent–subagent history follows costs into the wider human relational structure. Standard proofs and exact diagnostics clarify symmetry restrictions, capacity equivalence, propagation, historical reinforcement, replication, investment, and continuity. Matched-history comparisons distinguish possibilities, exercise, recipient response, and later standing, including complementarity, interference, copying, and selective records. The contribution is a connected comparative framework with explicit antecedents and evidence requirements. Community constitutions and interaction strategies remain configurable; implemented and field studies supply the subsequent assessment of their effects.
