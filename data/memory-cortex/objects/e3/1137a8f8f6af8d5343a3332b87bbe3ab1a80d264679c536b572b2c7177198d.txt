Characterization of the Evolutionary Stages of Medical Technology Implementation: Seven Case Studies

Characterization of the Evolutionary Stages of Medical Technology Implementation: Seven Case Studies At the beginning of this century, many scientists predicted that Man would fly to the moon; none of them, however, predicted an even more amazing event - that we would be able to sit in our living rooms and watch that first moon walk. Similarly, many technological advancements in medicine are easy to foresee, but predicting the technology that will really change our lives is nearly impossible. In the last 5 years, technologies that apply to many fields of medicine have been introduced. In medical school, there are now interactive CD-ROM'S and Internet-based references which add multimedia to the training of our new physicians. Virtual Reality has been applied in simulating surgery for training purposes, and in providing alternative communication means for the disabled. Telecommunication technologies allow additional surgeons to not only view, but participate via robotic arms in operations at distant sites. New diagnostic tools that use highly accurate sensors for earlier detection of diseases are being tested for many health problems, and will soon be accessible to all. Hand in hand with diagnostic testing is genetic mapping and testing which is making it possible for physicians to better understand the causes of many diseases. All of these technologies have the potential to benefit many people, but not all from even this short list will be widely implemented. For most of the years since the inception of the American health care system, the medical profession and the hospital field selected technology based upon the criteria: more, bigger, and faster. The focus of concern about technology began to change about twenty years ago. In this age of cost-based reimbursement, physicians have advocated investment in technologies which provide qualitative improvements in patient diagnostics and therapeutics. While this strategy has generally raised the level of care and quality of life of patients, it also led to a medical arms race between institutions. The public mood has also changed significantly. Throughout the 1970's, concern centered on ethical and humanitarian issues.. Means were sought to limit the unbridled use of life-extending technologies when there was little or no prospect of reasonable recovery. The right-to-die movement and the living will legislation were manifestations of this mood. Quite a different dilemma was posed by the emergence of renal dialysis, a technology that was usually successful in saving lives and that returned many patients to a reasonable quality of life for several years. The problem was cost because it emerged that patients who could benefit from this treatment were sometimes denied it because insurers could not or would not afford it. At the same time, health care costs as a whole were outstripping inflation and the cry went up that technology was the culprit. The 1980's were therefore dominated by attempts to control technology in order to cut costs - not much was heard about making the best use of technology. Now the emphasis has changed again - not because the problem of cost has been solved but because it has become so serious that the whole question of health care delivery is being reviewed. In the broader scope of health policy, one crucial issue is seen to be technology assessment . This assessment includes determination of relative benefits of different methods, as well as their hidden, non-financial costs. This arose with the realization that US health care may need to be rationed. For this rationing to be rational, there is a need to know not so much what different treatments cost, but whether they work. Perhaps the solution to the rationing issue lies in an examination of medical ethics. Answering to the four principles of ethics will help guide technology use decisions: 1. Beneficence - What is the probability that intervention will restore life of reasonable quality and duration? 2. Non-maleficence - What risk does the proposed intervention involve, and what stress will the patient have to endure? 3. Patient autonomy - Fully informed of this balance of probabilities, what is the patient's preference? 4. Justice - Does the expected benefit to this individual justify the cost in resources to the community? Rather than proposing a solution, in today's health care market, we can simply propose that not enough is known about how effective various technologies are when properly applied, and that even effective technologies are too often used inappropriately. It is this inappropriate high technology that we cannot afford, so we must search for and acquire technologies that are both necessary and have a reasonable likelihood of successfully achieving their objective. Medical technology is both the problem and the solution to providing improved access and quality, and decreased cost in health care. In this thesis, seven case studies are presented to exemplify the process of careful assessment leading to informed decisions.
