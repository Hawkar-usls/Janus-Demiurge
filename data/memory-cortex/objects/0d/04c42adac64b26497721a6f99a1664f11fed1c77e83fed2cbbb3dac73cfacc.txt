Bayesian Clinical Trials

This book is for the people who run clinical trials: investigators, physicians, biostatisticians at sponsors and contract research organizations, data monitoring committee members, and regulatory reviewers. It assumes an introductory statistics course and nothing more. The book starts from the traditional trial, with a fixed sample size, equal randomization, alpha spending for interim looks, and a p value and confidence interval in the study report. It then shows the Bayesian version of the same decision on the same numbers, and says plainly where the traditional design is the better choice. Regulation is the spine: each chapter names the statute, regulation or guidance that governs its topic, with its status and date. Our main claim is that a Bayesian adaptive design reaches the regulatory standard of evidence sooner without lowering the bar. Against a single unadjusted look the Bayesian bar is never lower without an informative prior. The saving comes from monitoring, from the handling of multiple arms, and from prior information, and we price each one with calibrated rules and simulated operating characteristics. We test this on real trials. In our reconstruction of the semaglutide trial SELECT, simulated at a true hazard ratio of 0.80, a calibrated Bayesian rule stops at a median of 486 events over all simulated trials (464 among those that declare efficacy), against 817 for SELECT's planned design (one interim analysis with Lan-DeMets spending, then the final), with power of 95 percent against 97 percent; an anytime valid evidence rule is slower and less powerful. The reconstruction is ours, not the sponsor's. We also put the cost of waiting in three currencies, the trial cost avoided, the cardiovascular label reached earlier inside a fixed patent life, and the health value inside and outside the trial, each a modeled counterfactual with its assumptions stated. A scheduled design can win when the effect is modest, as the dulaglutide trial REWIND shows. The opening chapters say why a sponsor would run a Bayesian trial, then cover hypothesis testing, randomization, the regulatory framework, and the life of a drug from first in human to postmarketing, with recruitment and the value of speed told through the 1995 Larry King appeal for prostate cancer families. The Bayesian basics are worked on trial data: the beta binomial on the 1965 trial of four duodenal ulcer operations and on the BNT162b2 vaccine success rule, the gamma Poisson for event rates, and normal normal shrinkage, followed by a chapter on hierarchical models and how much related groups should borrow from each other. Later chapters cover covariate adjustment, safety signals (rofecoxib, the gamma Poisson shrinker, toxicity stopping rules), time to event endpoints, noninferiority, and what trials can borrow from online experimentation. A chapter on Bayes in landmark trials goes through I-SPY 2, REMAP-CAP, the heparin multiplatform trial, BNT162b2, REBYOTA, GBM AGILE, DINAMO, DAWN and SNAP, giving for each the design, the decision rule, what happened and what regulators accepted. The book also reanalyzes GUSTO, EOLIA, ANDROMEDA SHOCK and RECOVERY, and describes trials running now, among them TRIUMPH 3 and Lp(a)HORIZON. A companion book, Personalized Medicine, turns from the trial to the patient. Every number comes from the companion R package bayestrials (https://github.com/VadimSokolov/bayestrials), an interactive lab at https://vsokolov.org/bayesian_trials_lab/ lets readers run the trials themselves, and an online encyclopedia at https://vsokolov.org/bayesian_trials_lab/encyclopedia/ links the methods, trials and documents to the sections of the book that treats it.
