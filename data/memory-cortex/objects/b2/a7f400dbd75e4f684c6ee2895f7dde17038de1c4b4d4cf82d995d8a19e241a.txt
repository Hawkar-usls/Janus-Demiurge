How likely is the flood?: A critical review of design-flood estimation for small pluvial agricultural catchments

Flood protection below small agricultural catchments is designed for discharges and volumes of a given return period that are rarely observed and therefore inferred from rainfall. This review asks how well the available methods support that inference where floods are generated by short, intense rainfall on cultivated land. It rests on published literature and Austrian guidance, not on new data. The evidence shows that the events of interest are local, last a few hours and are rarely recorded, and that their size is controlled jointly by rainfall, antecedent wetness, the state of the soil surface and the crop, and the connectivity of flow paths. The return period of a flood is therefore not the return period of its rainfall. Five families of methods, from design-storm calculations to continuous simulation and regional flood statistics, are compared by the assumption that maps rainfall probability to flood probability. Two analytical relations illustrate the consequences. With a variable runoff coefficient, a peak computed from the hundred-year rainfall and the mean coefficient has a return period of only six to forty years in the cases evaluated. With a threshold loss, flood frequency follows the probability that a storm meets a surface of low infiltration capacity. The review audits ten assumptions of current practice, proposes a decision scheme based on the data situation, and recommends combining independent evidence, treating the catchment state as a random variable, simulating continuously at sub-hourly resolution where storage matters, and designing retention and infiltration structures for volume, duration and initial filling.
