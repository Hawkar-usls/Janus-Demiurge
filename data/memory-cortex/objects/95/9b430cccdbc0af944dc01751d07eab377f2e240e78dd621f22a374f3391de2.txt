Inducing Extinctive Vortices in Cell Populations

Background. We propose subjecting specific cell populations to selective pressures analogous to reproductive competition observed among animals and other organisms, including mate harassment and sexual coercion. Classical oncology frequently confronts cancers with cytotoxic agents and maximal cell killing, imposing Darwinian selection that can ultimately contribute to the host’s demise; evolution itself has been identified as a proximate cause of death in most cancer patients (Enríquez-Navas et al., 2015). We hypothesize that exploiting existing cellular dependencies in replication-competent cells, in conjunction with their endogenous parasexual mechanisms, may drive those dependencies toward population-wide predominance. Through delivery by any suitable vector capable of inducing a pro-fusogenic state, the intervention may promote homotypic cell fusion and establish the reproductive pressures necessary to initiate the proposed extinctive vortices. Objective. The Induced Extinction Vortex (IEV) exploits endogenous reproductive mechanisms to drive local populations of replication-competent cells toward extinction through parasexual processes. Methods. The IEV is specified in three sequential phases: parasexualization, viable reproductive opportunity scarcity, and reversion. Each phase is defined by threshold conditions and explicit failure criteria. The proposal is method-agnostic; no particular delivery vehicle, parasexual driver, or receptor set is prescribed. Any implementation capable of instantiating the IEV as specified falls within the scope of this proposal. These predefined failure criteria will serve as benchmarks for subsequent research, mathematical modeling, and experimental assays. Supporting literature. Existing research documents spontaneous cancer-cell fusion and parasexual recombination, alongside demographic effects observed in natural populations, including mate limitation, reproductive interference, and inbreeding depression. Evolutionary oncology has also demonstrated that adaptive responses can be redirected into lethal liabilities. To our knowledge, however, no study has integrated these phenomena into a unified extinction strategy, particularly one directed toward populations composed predominantly of parasexual cells. Their convergence represents an open frontier in evolutionary oncology. Conclusions. The proposal hypothesizes that introducing genetic material capable of shifting a parasexual reproductive dependency toward population-wide predominance could establish both intraspecific and interspecific vulnerabilities. To our knowledge, existing research supports the individual components of this proposition, but their integration into a unified strategy has neither been documented nor experimentally evaluated.
