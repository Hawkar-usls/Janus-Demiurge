Protein Methyl-Group Quantum Tunneling Directly Detected by EPR

The researchers detected periodic oscillations in T m -edited DEER EPR signals from a model protein in which leucine methyl groups were protonated while the rest of the protein was deuterated. By combining a fully deuterated control with theoretical simulations, the researchers attributed the oscillations to methyl-tunneling electron spin echo envelope modulation and determined an average tunneling frequency of 0.12 ± 0.01 MHz. The experiments were performed on an isotopically engineered model protein at 50 K and do not demonstrate that living cells at physiological temperatures use methyl tunneling for biological function. [Quantum Biology Society] Quantum tunneling is often associated with electrons, protons, or hydrogen atoms crossing an energy barrier during a chemical reaction. But tunneling is not limited to motion along a reaction coordinate from one chemical state to another. It can also occur in small molecular groups such as methyl groups (-CH 3 ), which rotate around a chemical bond. At sufficiently high temperatures, a methyl group can acquire enough thermal energy to pass over its rotational energy barrier. At low temperatures, however, classical thermal rotation becomes strongly suppressed, and quantum mechanics allows states on different sides of the rotational barrier to remain connected through tunneling. In the theoretical description used in this study, a methyl rotor is represented by three localized rotational states corresponding to 0°, 120°, and 240°. A tunneling Hamiltonian couples these states. The methyl group is therefore not simply behaving like a freely rotating classical top; quantum coupling can connect rotational states separated by an energy barrier. Thomas Schmidt, Francesco Torricella, and G. Marius Clore of the Laboratory of Chemical Physics at the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), National Institutes of Health (NIH), directly detected this form of rotational quantum tunneling in leucine methyl groups within a protein using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). Using Staphylococcal protein A as a model, the researchers analyzed T m -edited double electron–electron resonance (DEER) EPR signals and obtained an average methyl-tunneling frequency of 0.12 ± 0.01 MHz. The paper was published online in 2026 and appeared in Chemical Communications , Volume 62, Issue 13, pages 4112–4116. ■ Reading Methyl Tunneling in Proteins Through EPR Oscillations Methyl tunneling has previously been studied in several organic and metallo-organic compounds using electron spin echo envelope modulation, or ESEEM. When methyl groups lie near an electron spin, hyperfine interactions between the nuclear spins of methyl protons and the unpaired electron can modulate the electron spin echo signal. If the methyl rotor tunnels among different localized rotational states, these interactions can generate a characteristic time-dependent modulation. Detecting the same effect inside a protein is more difficult. Proteins contain large numbers of hydrogen nuclei, which contribute to electron-spin decoherence and can shorten the phase memory time, potentially obscuring the subtle oscillatory signal produced by methyl tunneling. The researchers addressed this problem through isotope labeling. They prepared a version of protein A in which most hydrogen atoms were replaced with deuterium while the methyl groups of leucine residues remained protonated: {U[ 2 H]; Leu-[ 13 CH 3 ]}. The experimental solvent was also fully deuterated. This substantially suppressed proton-related background signals from the rest of the protein and solvent while selectively preserving the contribution from leucine methyl protons. ■ Two Distance States at 32 Å and 38 Å Between the Spin Labels The researchers introduced cysteines at two surface-exposed positions in protein A, Q39C and K88C, and attached the relatively rigid R1p nitroxide spin label to both sites. The R1p label at Q39C occupied two major conformational states. Conformer a was more exposed to solvent, whereas conformer b was packed closer to the protein surface. As a result, the DEER-derived electron–electron distance distribution between Q39C-R1p and K88C-R1p was bimodal. Conformer b produced a narrow distance peak at approximately 32 Å, while conformer a produced a broader peak at approximately 38 Å. These two structural populations became an important part of the strategy for detecting methyl tunneling. In conformer a, the distances between the unpaired electron of the Q39C spin label and leucine methyl carbon atoms were 15 Å or greater. In conformer b, in addition to more distant methyl groups, some methyl carbons approached the unpaired electron to within approximately 6–10 Å. Because the amplitude of methyl-tunneling ESEEM is highly sensitive to the distance between the unpaired electron and nearby methyl groups, comparing the time dependence of the two DEER distance peaks provided a way to enhance the contribution from leucine methyl groups located close to the spin label. ■ The Distance-Peak Ratio Oscillated Periodically at 50 K The researchers performed T m -edited DEER measurements under Q-band conditions at 50 K. They varied the second echo-period evolution time, T , and tracked the ratio of the integrated intensities of the approximately 32 Å and 38 Å distance peaks. In the leucine-methyl-protonated protein, this ratio did not simply decay smoothly with time. Instead, clear periodic oscillations appeared on top of an exponential decay. The researchers attributed these oscillations to methyl-tunneling ESEEM. Importantly, however, the observation of an oscillation alone was not sufficient to establish its origin. A separate isotope-control experiment was therefore used to test whether the signal actually arose from the protonated leucine methyl groups in protein A. ■ Removing Leucine Methyl Protons Removed the Oscillations The researchers repeated the experiment using a uniformly deuterated U[ 2 H] protein A control. In this sample, the leucine methyl protons were also replaced by deuterium. The difference was clear. In the sample containing protonated leucine methyl groups, the relative intensities of the 32 Å and 38 Å distance peaks oscillated as a function of evolution time. In the fully deuterated protein, no comparable oscillations were apparent up to T = 33 μs. The researchers also analyzed the ratio of the two distance-peak intensities rather than relying on the absolute intensity of either peak alone. This was important because the R1p spin label itself contains protonated methyl groups that could also contribute to ESEEM. Taking the ratio was designed to cancel the common modulation arising from those spin-label methyl groups. The experiment therefore used selective isotope labeling, a fully deuterated control, and a ratio-based analysis to isolate the oscillatory component associated with leucine methyl protons in protein A. ■ Reproducing the Experimental Oscillations with a Tunneling Hamiltonian To quantitatively interpret the oscillations, the researchers modeled the system with a spin Hamiltonian that explicitly included methyl tunneling. The model described each methyl rotor using three localized states at 0°, 120°, and 240°, coupled through an off-diagonal tunneling Hamiltonian. Atomic coordinates from protein A were then used to calculate the distances and orientations between the unpaired electron on Q39C-R1p and individual methyl groups. When the model used the experimentally observed conformers b and a, it reproduced both the period and amplitude of the experimental oscillations, with a normalized χ 2 value of 1.3. The researchers also tested a control structural model involving conformer c, a minor state predicted computationally but not observed experimentally in the DEER distance distribution. The conformer c/a model produced a substantially poorer fit, with χ 2 = 3.2. This comparison supports the interpretation that the observed modulation is linked not merely to an arbitrary combination of a methyl-tunneling model and spin-label geometry, but specifically to structural states consistent with those detected experimentally by DEER. ■ Average Methyl-Tunneling Frequency of 0.12 MHz Global fitting yielded an average methyl-tunneling frequency, ν t , of 0.12 ± 0.01 MHz. This number should not be interpreted as meaning that a methyl group is classically rotating 120,000 times per second. The parameter ν t is the characteristic tunneling frequency entering the Hamiltonian that couples the three localized rotational states of the methyl rotor. In the fitting procedure, the researchers assumed the same ν t for all methyl groups in protein A and identified the value that best reproduced the experimentally observed oscillation pattern. The measured 0.12 MHz therefore does not represent a direct rotational rate for one particular leucine side chain. Rather, it is an average tunneling parameter describing the ensemble of methyl groups sensed in this protein system. The researchers note that this value lies toward the lower end of methyl-tunneling frequencies previously determined experimentally by ESEEM or predicted theoretically for organic and metallo-organic model compounds. They interpret the relatively low tunneling frequency as indicative of a comparatively high rotational barrier for methyl groups in protein A. ■ Nearby Methyl Groups Dominated the Signal, but All Methyl Groups Were Needed Structural analysis indicated that methyl groups located especially close to the unpaired electron made the largest contributions to the observed oscillations. In conformer b, several methyl carbon atoms were located approximately 6–10 Å from the spin-label electron. The authors concluded that tunneling of methyl groups located significantly less than 10 Å from the unpaired nitroxide electron was the major contributor to the experimental modulation. However, including only the closest methyl groups was not sufficient to fully reproduce the observed signal. Accurate simulation of both the amplitude and frequency of the oscillations required the contributions of all methyl groups in the protein to be included. The reported 0.12 MHz value should therefore be understood as an average methyl-tunneling frequency derived from the combined influence of multiple methyl groups within the protein structure rather than as a site-specific measurement of one individual leucine residue. ■ This Does Not Mean "Life Uses Tunneling" The authors describe the study as the first direct observation of methyl tunneling in a protein by EPR. Here, "direct observation" does not mean that the physical rotation of a methyl group was visually imaged in real space. It means that the researchers detected a characteristic EPR oscillation in a protein containing protonated methyl groups, showed that the oscillation disappeared in the corresponding fully deuterated control, and quantitatively reproduced its time dependence using a Hamiltonian that explicitly includes quantum methyl tunneling. The experiment was also not performed in living cells. It used a carefully isotope-labeled model protein, and the key T m -edited DEER measurements were carried out at 50 K. The results therefore do not demonstrate that organisms at physiological temperatures use methyl tunneling as a biological mechanism, nor do they establish that protein function is controlled by methyl tunneling. What the study directly shows is that rotational quantum tunneling can be detected within a protein environment by EPR and that its average characteristic frequency can be quantitatively extracted. This phenomenon should also be distinguished from the hydrogen or proton tunneling commonly discussed in enzyme reactions. Hydrogen-transfer tunneling often concerns motion of a particle along a chemical reaction coordinate from one state to another. The methyl tunneling measured here instead concerns the rotational degree of freedom of a methyl rotor moving quantum mechanically among rotational states without breaking existing chemical bonds or forming new ones. The researchers further propose that T m -edited DEER could eventually be useful not only for determining average methyl-tunneling frequencies but also for distinguishing conformational states occupied by rigid spin labels or testing structural states predicted from protein atomic coordinates.
