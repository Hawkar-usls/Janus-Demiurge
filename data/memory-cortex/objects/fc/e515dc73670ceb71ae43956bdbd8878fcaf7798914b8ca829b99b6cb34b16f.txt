Arabidopsis Photosynthetic Fluorescence Changes Within 10 Seconds of a Near-Null Magnetic-Field Transition

When Arabidopsis thaliana was shifted for 10 seconds from the normal geomagnetic field of about 45 μT to a near-null magnetic field of about 30 nT, OJIP chlorophyll-fluorescence amplitudes rapidly increased. Under continued exposure to the altered field, PAM measurements showed reduced PSII operating efficiency and enhanced non-photochemical energy dissipation. Experiments using photosynthetic mutants and the PSII perturbants DCMU and NH 2 OH produced different response patterns, indicating that the magnitude and expression of the magnetic-field-dependent fluorescence phenotype are influenced by PSII functional state, electron transport, photoprotection, and antenna organization. The study did not directly detect a specific radical pair or molecular magnetosensor, and its simplified spin-Hamiltonian analysis was intended only as a conceptual assessment of potentially compatible timescales. [Quantum Biology Society] The magnetic field at Earth's surface is typically on the order of tens of microtesla, extremely weak compared with the thermal fluctuations experienced by biomolecules. Yet studies have continued to suggest that changes in magnetic-field environments may influence not only animal navigation but also plant growth, metabolism, reactive oxygen species regulation, and photosynthesis. In plants, the blue-light receptor cryptochrome and the radical pair mechanism (RPM) are among the leading candidates for explaining magnetic sensitivity. However, in an intact living plant, it remains unclear which physiological process responds first to a weak magnetic-field change and where the initial molecular sensing event actually occurs. Alice Olmo, Chidananda Nagamangala Kanchiswamy, and Massimo E. Maffei of the University of Turin investigated this question by rapidly shifting Arabidopsis thaliana between the geomagnetic field (GMF), a near-null magnetic field (NNMF), and a stronger hypermagnetic field (HMF), while monitoring photosynthetic responses. The researchers moved plants from a GMF of approximately 45 μT to an NNMF of approximately 30 nT for 10 seconds and then measured chlorophyll fluorescence while the leaves remained inside the altered magnetic environment. They also used several photosynthetic mutants and pharmacological perturbations of Photosystem II (PSII) to determine how the observed magnetic-field response depended on the physiological state of the photosynthetic apparatus. The paper was received on July 27, 2026, accepted on October 6, 2026, and is currently available as an Article in Press in Scientific Reports . ■ Nearly Canceling the Geomagnetic Field Rapidly Increased OJIP Fluorescence The researchers used a triaxial Helmholtz-coil system to compensate for the local geomagnetic field of approximately 45 μT along three spatial axes, reducing the residual magnetic field to about 30 nT. A separate HMF condition was approximately 1 mT. After 10 seconds of exposure to each magnetic condition, the leaves remained inside the same magnetic environment while chlorophyll fluorescence was measured. The first measurement used the OJIP chlorophyll-fluorescence induction curve. OJIP analysis records the successive O, J, I, and P phases of fluorescence that emerge as the redox state of electron acceptors changes following illumination of PSII. In wild-type Arabidopsis , transition to NNMF produced a clear increase in absolute fluorescence amplitudes. In the Discussion, the researchers summarized that several OJIP fluorescence levels increased by approximately 30–40% relative to GMF controls. However, F v / F m , which represents the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII, did not significantly change in wild-type plants. This distinction is important. The result does not suggest that PSII was rapidly damaged and lost its maximum photochemical capacity. Instead, the dominant immediate effect was associated with changes in fluorescence yield and the distribution or dissipation of excitation energy. The researchers also tested whether sample handling or the measurement apparatus itself could account for the effect. In sham experiments, plants underwent the same transfer, positioning, and handling procedures without changing the magnetic-field intensity. No statistically significant OJIP or PAM changes were detected. Small field-dependent differences were observed in some measurements using chlorophyll extracts and heat-killed leaves. However, the estimated contribution of these passive or instrumental effects to the F m and F v changes seen in living plants was generally below 5%. The researchers therefore concluded that the large NNMF-associated fluorescence changes in intact leaves were unlikely to be explained simply by optical artifacts or altered fluorometer performance. ■ Continued NNMF Exposure Reduced Photosynthetic Efficiency and Increased Photoprotection The researchers next used pulse-amplitude-modulated fluorescence, or PAM, to examine PSII behavior under continuous actinic illumination. The timescale of this experiment needs to be distinguished from that of the rapid OJIP measurement. OJIP fluorescence mainly captured the state of the photosynthetic system roughly 10–12 seconds after the magnetic-field transition. During PAM measurements, by contrast, samples remained under the altered magnetic environment for approximately 150–200 seconds. PAM therefore may reflect not only the immediate response but also photosynthetic regulatory processes developing during continued exposure. Under NNMF, the effective quantum yield of PSII, QY L1 , decreased. Photochemical quenching, q P , which reflects the fraction of open PSII reaction centers, also decreased. In contrast, non-photochemical quenching, NPQ, increased. These changes indicate that the balance between photochemical energy conversion and protective energy dissipation shifted under the altered magnetic environment. A particularly clear difference appeared in the rapidly reversible, energy-dependent component of non-photochemical quenching, q E . In wild-type plants, NPQ E was 0.38 ± 0.04 under GMF but increased to 0.79 ± 0.31 under NNMF, approximately doubling. Under the approximately 1 mT HMF condition, the value remained near the GMF level at 0.37 ± 0.04. NNMF and HMF therefore did not produce symmetrical responses. Reducing the magnetic field almost to zero produced a pronounced photosynthetic response, whereas increasing the field to approximately 1 mT produced comparatively modest effects. This suggests that the response is unlikely to be a simple linear function of the magnitude of the magnetic-field change. The researchers nevertheless did not interpret the fluorescence data as direct evidence that a specific electron-transfer step had slowed or that the proton motive force across the thylakoid membrane had changed. Chlorophyll fluorescence alone cannot directly measure quantities such as plastoquinone redox state, individual electron-transfer rates, or proton motive force. Those mechanisms therefore remain possibilities rather than demonstrated explanations. ■ Changing Photosynthetic Mutants Also Changed the Expression of the Magnetic Response To investigate which photosynthetic processes influence the NNMF phenotype, the researchers compared several Arabidopsis mutants. The ch1 mutant lacks chlorophyll b and consequently has a strongly reduced PSII antenna system. npq1 is impaired in the xanthophyll cycle. npq4 lacks the PsbS protein required for rapid energy-dependent quenching. pgr5 is impaired in cyclic electron flow around Photosystem I. All of these mutants showed photosynthetic fluorescence responses following transition to NNMF, but the magnitude and pattern of those responses differed. The ch1 mutant showed particularly clear changes. After transition to NNMF, F v / F m decreased from 0.75 ± 0.01 to 0.72 ± 0.01. The electron-transport-related quantum yield Φ E0 and the photosynthetic performance index PI ABS also decreased. However, ch1 already has a substantially different antenna architecture and baseline photosynthetic state from wild-type plants. The researchers therefore caution against directly comparing response amplitudes and interpreting the result as evidence that chlorophyll b or a specific antenna component functions as a magnetic sensor. Differences were also observed in npq1 , npq4 , and pgr5 , although many genotype-specific contrasts did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. The authors' conclusion is therefore not that these genes directly detect magnetic fields. Rather, physiological processes such as cyclic electron flow, photoprotective regulation, and antenna organization appear to influence how an initial magnetic-field perturbation is buffered, amplified, or ultimately expressed as a measurable fluorescence phenotype. ■ Perturbing Both Sides of PSII Also Altered the Response Pattern The researchers also pharmacologically perturbed the electron-acceptor and electron-donor sides of PSII. DCMU binds at the Q B site of PSII and blocks electron transfer from the primary quinone acceptor Q A to the secondary acceptor Q B . Hydroxylamine, NH 2 OH, disrupts the Mn cluster of the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC), interfering with water oxidation on the donor side of PSII. Both treatments altered the fluorescence response to NNMF. In DCMU-treated leaves, NNMF was associated with significant changes in several OJIP-derived parameters, including F o , Ψ 0 , and Φ E0 . NH 2 OH-treated leaves also showed changes in both OJIP and PAM fluorescence parameters after NNMF exposure. However, both compounds profoundly alter the normal physiological state of PSII. The results therefore cannot establish that "the magnetic sensor lies between Q A and Q B " or that "the oxygen-evolving complex senses the magnetic field." The study's Figure 4 summarizes this experimental logic by mapping the perturbations onto the photosynthetic electron-transport chain. It places DCMU and NH 2 OH at their respective sites of action and shows the ch1 , npq1 , npq4 , pgr5 , ATX1, and ISCA3-related pathways alongside the chain extending from PSII through the plastoquinone pool, cytochrome b 6 f, Photosystem I, and ferredoxin. The significance of this strategy is not that it identifies one magnetic sensor. Instead, it asks how disrupting different parts of the photosynthetic network changes the NNMF-associated phenotype, thereby narrowing the physiological context in which the response is expressed. ■ Radical Pairs Are One Possible Explanation, but Direct Evidence Is Still Missing The primary photochemistry of PSII contains multiple charge-separated states and radical-pair intermediates. According to the timescales discussed in the paper, the initially formed P D1 •+ Pheo D1 •− state is stabilized by electron transfer to Q A in approximately 400 ps. Reduction of P D1 •+ by Tyr Z occurs over approximately 50–250 ns, depending on the S-state. Electron transfer from Q A •− to Q B takes approximately 0.2–0.4 ms. In the radical pair mechanism, spin-correlated radical pairs can evolve between singlet and triplet character, and external magnetic fields can influence this spin evolution. In principle, this can alter charge recombination or subsequent reaction branching. To ask whether magnetic-field-dependent spin evolution could occur on timescales physically compatible with photosynthetic reactions, the researchers introduced a simplified quantum spin-Hamiltonian framework. Considering only the additional electron Zeeman energy associated with a change in field strength, the transition from GMF to NNMF, corresponding to Δ B of approximately 45 μT, gave a characteristic time of about 1.26 × 10 −7 s, or approximately 126 ns, for accumulating an additional phase difference of roughly one radian. For a GMF-to-HMF difference of approximately 1 mT, the corresponding estimate was approximately 5.67 ns. These numbers require careful interpretation. They are not measurements of quantum coherence times. They are also not measurements or predictions of radical-pair lifetimes, singlet-triplet interconversion times, reaction yields, or fluorescence changes. The authors explicitly describe these values as heuristic timescale estimates. Real radical-pair dynamics depend on the complete spin Hamiltonian, including hyperfine interactions, exchange coupling, electron-electron dipolar coupling, spin relaxation, loss of spin correlation, and competing chemical reaction rates. The calculation therefore establishes only that magnetic-field-dependent spin-phase evolution cannot be excluded on timescale grounds alone, because the estimated times overlap with some photosynthetic reaction timescales. It does not demonstrate that such spin dynamics caused the fluorescence response observed in the plants. ■ This Is Not a Discovery of a "PSII Magnetosensor" One of the strengths of the study is its focus on rapid responses. Rather than exposing plants to altered magnetic fields for hours or days and then measuring growth, metabolism, or gene expression, the researchers began by examining photosynthetic changes following only a 10-second transition. The purpose was to look for physiological responses occurring before longer-term transcriptional, metabolic, developmental, or acclimatory processes could dominate the phenotype. However, describing the study as the discovery of PSII as a plant magnetoreceptor would go beyond the evidence. The genetic and pharmacological experiments show that the NNMF-associated fluorescence phenotype depends on the physiological state of PSII and the surrounding photosynthetic network. They do not identify the molecular site at which the initial magnetic-field-sensitive event occurs. The authors discuss spin-dependent PSII radical-pair reactions as one possible mechanism, but they also acknowledge other possibilities, including redox regulation, membrane organization, proton-motive-force dynamics, and broader network-level responses. The experimental design also has important limitations. Only one NNMF condition, approximately 30 nT, one HMF condition, approximately 1 mT, and one initial 10-second exposure duration were examined. In addition, OJIP and PAM measurements probe different temporal windows. OJIP predominantly captures the rapid state approximately 10–12 seconds after transition, whereas PAM includes regulatory responses that develop while the sample remains under the altered magnetic field for approximately 150–200 seconds. The study therefore should not be interpreted as showing that "the entire PSII electron-transfer system changes within 10 seconds." More direct experiments will be required to establish the molecular mechanism. These include time-resolved spectroscopy of relevant radical pairs and charge-recombination pathways, direct measurements of reactive oxygen species and triplet formation, and measurements of electron-transfer kinetics, redox states, and proton motive force under controlled magnetic fields. The most appropriate interpretation is consequently more restrained. The study shows that a rapid transition to a near-null magnetic field can be accompanied by measurable changes in chlorophyll fluorescence, photochemical operating efficiency, and photoprotective regulation in living Arabidopsis , and that the expression of these changes depends strongly on the physiological state of PSII and the wider photosynthetic network.
