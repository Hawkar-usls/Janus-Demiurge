PHANTOM Analysis of Planet Candidate TIC 233191240 (Lastovka et al. 2026): A 3.81627-Day Transiting Giant-Planet Candidate Around an M Dwarf in TESS Photometry

Status: planet candidate, first reported by Lastovka et al. (2026); a pair of near-equal M dwarfs at twice the period is disfavored but not excluded; if single, its mass is unmeasured and a brown dwarf or a very-low-mass star of the same size is not excluded. A periodic transit-like signal on TIC 233191240 detected independently by PHANTOM (Planet Hunting Across Noisy TESS Observations and Measurements), an independent search of NASA TESS photometry. It was first reported as a planet candidate by Lastovka et al. (2026; arXiv:2610.28518, Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.23504163), posted on arXiv and Zenodo on 20 October 2026, before PHANTOM's search detected it (21 November 2026) and 33 days before this record's first version (22 November 2026), which did not cite it; this record is an independent detection and analysis of their candidate. They list it with TRICERATOPS FPP 0.3756 and NFPP 0.1579, values their paper gives as a reference from runs that used no contrast curves or ground-based data; this record's own runs and difference images bear on the nearby stars (see False-positive probability and Location of the dip). Grouped timing fits to this record's TESS light curve favor its ephemeris over theirs: theirs matches in 2019-2020 and predicts transits 19 to 49 minutes early in 2021-2024 (see Prior detections); the other differences between the two analyses are not reconciled here. It has not been confirmed as a planet: a pair of near-equal M dwarfs at twice the period is disfavored but not excluded, and if single, its mass is unmeasured (see Unresolved). Earlier detections: Lastovka et al. 2026 (Lastovka, Barclay, Wilson & Gilbert 2026, Two Validated Planets and 179 Candidates From a TESS Faint Star Transit Search in the ULTRASAT Fields, arXiv:2610.28518; tables on Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.23504163): reported this signal as a planet candidate (its candidate table, and its TCE table with disposition PC), posted on arXiv and Zenodo on 20 October 2026, before PHANTOM's detection (21 November 2026) and this record's first version (22 November 2026), which did not cite it: P = 3.8161825 d (no uncertainty given), T0 = BTJD 1684.22314, depth 14,636 ppm, duration 1.22 h, 8.84 Earth radii, FPP 0.3756, NFPP 0.1579. It is the earliest public report of this signal as a planet candidate that we identified; this record is an independent detection and analysis of it. PHANTOM's cross-match did not include Lastovka et al.'s tables until 27 November 2026, and the first cross-match of that day missed this row (its blank uncertainty columns); neither the outside search of 22 November nor PHANTOM's own searches found their tables. The two ephemerides differ: their period is 0.0000898 d shorter than this record's, so their predicted transit times run from about 5 minutes later than this record's in 2019 to about 51 minutes earlier at the last sectors here (74-76, early 2024) and about 85 minutes earlier by late November 2026, most of the 1.71-hour transit. Fitted in five groups of sectors (analysis__timing_lastovka_20018.py and its .json: the masked light curve, one common time shift per group of a trapezoid with this record's depth and duration, its ingress fraction and baseline held fixed and not integrated over the exposures, white-noise errors), the best-fitting offsets from this record's ephemeris are -2.0 to +2.0 minutes in every group from 2019 to 2024, and the transits fall 6 and 0 minutes from theirs in 2019 and 2020 and 19, 28 and 49 minutes later than theirs predicts in sectors 40-41, 47-49 and 74-76 (2021, 2022 and early 2024). This record's ephemeris was fitted to the same light curve, so agreement with it is expected: this is an internal check, not independent confirmation or a two-minute bound on future transit times, and the test is of their ephemeris. Its intervals omit correlated noise and template uncertainty, but the 19-49-minute offsets are far outside them. Observers should use this record's ephemeris, propagating its uncertainty and allowing extra baseline (*For follow-up observers*). Their depth (14,636 ppm) is shallower than this record's (18,034 ± 859 ppm), their duration shorter (1.22 against 1.71 h) and their planet radius smaller (8.84 against 9.33 Earth radii), with nearly identical stellar radii (0.630 solar radii in their table, 0.629 here). Their FPP (0.3756) lies within the range of this record's controlled runs with the neighbors kept (0.0832-0.4372 across its two set-ups; its automated run gave 0.088); their NFPP (0.1579) is higher than any of those runs' (0.00711-0.0543), and this record's NFPP of zero after excluding three neighbors rests on its difference-image assessment (*Location of the dip*). The tables alone do not establish the reason for the differences in depth, duration, radius and NFPP, which are not reconciled here. (README.md, Prior detections). The full record, with the evidence, uncertainties, data sources, cross-matches, unresolved questions and publication history, is README.md in this deposit.
