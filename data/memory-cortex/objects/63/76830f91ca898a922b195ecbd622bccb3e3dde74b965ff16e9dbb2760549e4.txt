One Tree, Two Fluids: Convergent Mathematical Architecture of River Deltas and Arterial Trees

This paper argues that river deltas and arterial trees, though studied in separate fields, share a common mathematical architecture. Using side-by-side images as a Rosetta stone, it treats their resemblance as a diagnostic clue rather than a metaphor. Both are described as rooted, planar, directed, space-filling trees with a single inlet, repeated branching, and many terminal outlets. At every internal junction, flow is conserved. Both exhibit hierarchical scaling and self-similarity. The central contribution is a common variational template. If a branching network minimizes a separable cost combining transport dissipation and maintenance or volume cost, then optimal branch sizes at each junction obey a power-law relation. The exponent depends on the physics: viscous laminar flow yields Murray's law for blood vessels, while Manning-style open-channel flow yields a different, testable river scaling. Thus the template does not prove identity; it shows that one abstract optimization structure can generate known biological scaling and a conditional geophysical prediction. The paper then asks why such similarity exists. It offers conjectures: convergent optimization, constructal law, diffusion-limited aggregation, resource constraints, robustness, and a universal attractor. It states falsification tests: scaling exponents and graph metrics should not be random after conditioning on constraints, and diagnostics should transfer across domains. A second theoretical part connects the networks to thin and thick chaos. It defines a diagnostic tuple and proves transport-graph invariance: if two transport graphs are isomorphic in the relevant sense, preserving coherent and failure regions and the ordering of edge weights, then thin, transitional, and thick classifications transfer. This supports cross-domain diagnostics for flood forecasting and cardiovascular risk, for example, tracking failure pathways. The paper is careful about limits. Rivers and arteries differ physically in fluid, compliance, timescale, and dynamics. The claim is structural and mathematical, not physical identity. The unity is a shared abstract architecture, not sameness.
