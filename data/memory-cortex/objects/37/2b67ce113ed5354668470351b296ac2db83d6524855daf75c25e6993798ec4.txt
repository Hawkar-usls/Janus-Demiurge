The Last Human Buffer: Why AI-Era Layoffs May Make Firms Less Resilient to Geoeconomic Shocks

AI-driven workforce reductions may improve routine productivity while removing human capabilities needed during geopolitical disruption. This integrative conceptual article asks when AI-enabled restructuring undermines, preserves or improves organisational recoverability. It defines the human continuity buffer (HCB) as five independently mobilisable, pre-shock options: exception expertise, cross-role backup, independent verification, relational alternatives and executable decision authority. Eight propositions are developed, with the primary hypothesis predicting that HCB is more strongly associated with lower subsequent service loss in highly AI-dependent operations. A prospective empirical design specifies abrupt external export-control changes as the primary geoeconomic shock class and separates three questions: whether restructuring changes HCB, whether HCB predicts later recovery, and whether HCB mediates a causal restructuring effect. The proposed primary HCB measure applies a scenario-specific bottleneck coverage rule, reports all five dimensions separately, and is evaluated against prespecified resilience, human-capital and crisis-readiness benchmarks. Competing liquidity and process-simplification benefits are analysed without treating post-restructuring reserves as baseline confounders. A continuity-adjusted productivity protocol compares staffing designs without assigning unvalidated weights. The paper is a conceptual synthesis, not an empirical demonstration: no recovery effects, validated HCB scale or general anti-layoff claim are asserted. It offers a testable account of why normal-period efficiency and shock-period recoverability may diverge.
